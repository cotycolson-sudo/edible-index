About This Project

How do you eat a library?

This question is both literal and metaphorical. The Edible Index explores cookbooks as cultural touchstones—not just as recipe collections, but as primary sources that preserve heritage, encode philosophy, and carry memory across generations and oceans.

Each essay here examines a cookbook alongside the lived experience of cooking from it. The focus is not on whether recipes "work" or whether books are "good," but on what these texts reveal about culture, identity, faith, politics, and the ways food intersects with all of them.

Access Points

I come to food through unexpected doorways. With Korean food, it was residence life—making weekly H-Mart trips with exchange students as an RA, buying rice cookers and enormous jars of kimchi, learning cuisine through the grocery shopping of people making home in a foreign place.

With Indian food, it was religion—the Hare Krishna movement in Chicago, temple kitchens where food was prasadam, offered first to Krishna and then shared communally. I learned that cooking could be devotion, that intention mattered as much as technique.

With Chinese food, it was vegetarianism. I needed to understand how Mandarin and Cantonese cuisines treated vegetables with sophistication and respect that Western cooking rarely offered. Simple steamed gai lan became a revelation.

These access points matter. They shape what I learn and how I learn it. I didn't discover these cuisines through travel or restaurant recommendations—I discovered them through lived experience, through community, through necessity. The essays here reflect that approach: food as entry point into understanding culture, memory, and identity.

The Library

Most of the cookbooks featured here come from public libraries. This is intentional. The public availability of these texts matters—which cuisines are represented in library collections, which authors are published and distributed, which culinary traditions are deemed worthy of documentation and preservation.

I check out cookbooks the way others might check out novels: multiple at a time, reading them cover-to-cover, flagging recipes to attempt. Then I cook. I document. I write about what these books reveal—not just about food, but about the cultures and communities they represent.

Approach & Method

This is research through cooking. Each essay involves:

  • Reading the cookbook as a complete text, not just a recipe collection
  • Researching the author, cultural context, and historical background
  • Cooking multiple recipes from the book over weeks or months
  • Documenting technique, ingredients, process, results
  • Writing analytical essays that treat food as a lens for understanding culture

The goal is not to produce "cookbook reviews" but to explore the intersection of food, culture, heritage, memory, faith, and identity. Each cookbook is a cultural artifact. Each recipe is a preserved moment. Each meal is research.

Who I Am

I'm Coty, a data analytics student at UW Tacoma working at the intersection of AI, business analytics, and research. I approach food writing the way I approach data analysis: with rigor, attention to detail, and a commitment to understanding systems and patterns.

My background includes residence life work with international students, involvement with faith communities centered around food, and a long practice of vegetarian cooking that pushed me to learn techniques from multiple culinary traditions. These experiences shape how I approach cookbooks—as texts that encode not just recipes but entire systems of thought about ingredients, technique, time, and transformation.
