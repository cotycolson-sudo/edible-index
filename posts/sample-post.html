The First Jar of Kimchi: Access Points and Arrival

From: Korean Home Cooking by Sohui Kim January 28, 2026

Napa cabbage kimchi, three days into fermentation

I came to Korean food through residence life, not restaurants. My first year as an RA, I had seventeen Korean exchange students on my floor, and by October we were making weekly trips to H-Mart in a borrowed van. They needed rice cookers and aloe beverages and enormous jars of things I'd never encountered—bright red gochugaru, golden-brown doenjang, and always, always kimchi.

This is important: I didn't discover Korean food through travel or through a particularly good restaurant recommendation. I discovered it through the grocery shopping of people trying to make home in a foreign place. The access point matters. It shapes what you learn and how you learn it.

When Sohui Kim writes in Korean Home Cooking that "kimchi is more than a side dish—it's a way of thinking about time and transformation," she's talking about fermentation, yes, but also about the way food carries memory across oceans. My students weren't just buying kimchi. They were buying continuity. They were buying the particular sharp-sour-spicy taste of home.

"Every jar of kimchi is a small act of defiance against forgetting."

I started making my own kimchi because one of my students, Jiwon, asked if I'd ever tried it fresh—not the refrigerated kind from the store, but the kind you make yourself and let ferment on your counter for three days before the bubbles start. I hadn't. She wrote down her grandmother's method on a Post-it note: salt the cabbage, rinse it, pack it with seasoning paste, wait.

The waiting is the thing. American cooking rarely asks you to wait three days for something to become itself. We want immediate transformation—heat applied, dinner ready. But kimchi operates on a different timeline. You mix the paste, you pack the jar, and then you leave it alone. You trust the bacteria to do their work.

This is where Kim's cookbook becomes essential. She doesn't just give you a recipe for kimchi—she gives you a framework for understanding why Koreans make dozens of different kinds. Napa cabbage kimchi (baechu kimchi) for winter. Radish kimchi (kkakdugi) for soup. Water kimchi (mul kimchi) for summer. Each one a response to season, to availability, to the specific hunger of a moment.

What I learned from making kimchi alongside those Post-it notes and Kim's book is that fermentation is a form of patience that our food culture has largely abandoned. We want consistency, predictability, shelf-stability achieved through pasteurization rather than through the living preservation of fermentation. But kimchi asks you to accept variation. It asks you to understand that every batch will taste slightly different depending on temperature and time and the particular populations of bacteria in your kitchen.

My first batch was too salty. My second batch didn't ferment enough—I'd put it in the fridge too early, nervous about the bubbling. My third batch was perfect, and I understood why Jiwon had asked me to try it. The flavor was bright and alive in a way the store-bought versions never were. It tasted like it was still changing, which it was.

This is what cookbooks do at their best: they don't just teach you to cook a dish, they teach you to think in the logic of a cuisine. Kim's book taught me that Korean cooking is fundamentally about fermentation, about preserving things in ways that make them more interesting than they were when they started. Soy sauce, doenjang, gochujang, kimchi—all of them products of carefully managed decay, carefully cultivated transformation.

Years later, I still make kimchi every few weeks. I've graduated from Post-it notes to Kim's detailed instructions, which include the revelation that you should save your kimchi juice to use in stews and soups, that nothing should be wasted, that every stage of fermentation offers something useful.

The access point matters. I came to Korean food through the grocery shopping of people making home abroad, and that shaped everything I learned afterward. I learned it as everyday food, as the thing you make in big batches and keep in the fridge and eat with everything. I learned it as something generous and practical and absolutely central to daily life.

Every jar of kimchi is a small act of defiance against forgetting. It says: these flavors matter, this process matters, this way of making food is worth preserving even when you're far from home. That's what my students were buying at H-Mart, and that's what I learned to make in my own kitchen. Not just a side dish, but a whole philosophy of preservation and change.

Napa Cabbage Kimchi (Baechu Kimchi)

Adapted from Korean Home Cooking by Sohui Kim

Ingredients

  • 2 medium napa cabbages (about 5 lbs total)
  • 1 cup coarse sea salt
  • 1 cup gochugaru (Korean red pepper flakes)
  • 1/4 cup fish sauce
  • 1/4 cup salted shrimp (saeujeot), minced
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 daikon radish, julienned
  • 4 scallions, cut into 2-inch pieces
  • 1/2 cup garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons fresh ginger, minced
  • 1/4 cup sweet rice flour mixed with 1 cup water (cooled porridge)

Method

  1. Cut cabbages lengthwise into quarters. Dissolve salt in 1 gallon water. Soak cabbage in brine for 4-6 hours, turning occasionally.
  2. Make porridge: cook rice flour and water together until thick, then cool completely.
  3. Rinse cabbage thoroughly under cold water. Drain well.
  4. Make seasoning paste: combine cooled porridge, gochugaru, fish sauce, salted shrimp, sugar, garlic, and ginger.
  5. Add radish and scallions to paste. Mix well.
  6. Wearing gloves, rub paste between cabbage leaves, working from outer leaves inward.
  7. Pack tightly into jars or container, pressing down to remove air pockets.
  8. Leave at room temperature for 2-3 days (less in summer, more in winter) until fermentation begins.
  9. Transfer to refrigerator. Kimchi will continue to ferment slowly and is best after 1 week.

Notes

The timing of fermentation depends entirely on your kitchen temperature. In summer, check after 24 hours. In winter, it might need 4 days. You're looking for bubbles and a sour smell—that's how you know the fermentation has begun.

Save the juice that accumulates in your kimchi container. It's excellent added to stews, soups, or used as a marinade base.

About the Cookbook

Korean Home Cooking

by Sohui Kim

Abrams, 2018 • 304 pages

Sohui Kim's Korean Home Cooking is less interested in restaurant-style Korean food than in the everyday dishes that structure daily life. Her recipes assume you're making big batches, that you'll have banchan (side dishes) in your fridge, that you understand cooking as an ongoing practice rather than discrete events. It's a generous, practical book that treats readers as capable of understanding fermentation, time, and transformation.
